What does a space pony actually look like?

The vertical slice runs on gray-box capsules — physics is real, art isn't, on purpose. Genetics can warp a pony's leg count, tail type and silhouette across generations, so "pony" is a vibe, not a body plan. Below are three honest, fully-realized directions for what that vibe could look like on screen. Pick one, or steal pieces from all three.

the reskin nobody's picked yet

Three directions, three different bets

Each one commits to a different silhouette, a different production cost, and a different reason it should feel wholesome instead of weird-for-weird's-sake. None of them is the "safe" choice — that's rather the point of exploring this in parallel with engineering.

Direction one

Faceted Frontier

featured mutation: 3 rocket-boot legs, antigrav horn

Flat-shaded, low-vertex geometry in the spirit of PS1-era 3D and vertex-lit Katamari-adjacent worlds — chunky and jagged enough that it reads as goofy on sight, never sinister. Low-poly silhouettes already look like they're vibrating at low frame rates, so the sine-wave wobble the controller adds basically sells itself instead of fighting the model. It's also the cheapest of the three for a small Godot team: simple meshes, a single flat/toon shader, and new breeding body-plans (swap a leg count, re-facet a horn) are fast blockouts rather than full re-sculpts.

Godot build effort low
Direction two

The Doughnaut

featured mutation: 4 noodle-tentacle legs, third eye

Hand-pinched clay with visible thumbprints, lopsided proportions and soft matte shading, in the spirit of Aardman and Laika stop-motion. This is the direction where "janky by design" stops needing an excuse — a wobbly, imperfect surface is exactly what real clay looks like, so the procedural bob reads as material, not as a bug. It's also the hardest sell for a small team: faking clay convincingly in real-time (matte sub-surface look, dimple texture, asymmetric silhouettes) needs real shader work, and every new bred body-plan is closer to a fresh sculpt than a simple mesh swap.

Godot build effort high
Direction three

Cutout Cosmos

featured mutation: paper wings, stacked flame rocket-tail

Bold flat color, thick ink outlines and offset "paper layer" drop-shadows, closer to collage/cutout animation than 3D at all. Candy-bright and blunt-shaped, it lands wholesome-absurd through sheer cheerfulness and reads instantly at a glance during a frantic four-way race — exactly when a busier style would turn to noise. It's the cheapest and fastest of the three to produce and reskin per breeding trait (flat meshes or billboards, no complex shading), but flat shapes can look pasted-on in a physics-driven 3D track unless the team commits to billboarding or careful faceting to keep it feeling embedded in the world rather than stickered onto it.

Godot build effort low–medium

Three ways to build Faceted Frontier

"Low-poly" isn't one look — it's a whole family, from PS1 mascot games to Minecraft to a scratched 1998 demo disc. These three riffs commit fully to the same rig as Direction One (angular facets, the three mismatched rocket-boot legs, the glowing antigrav horn) and just change what happens at render time — a closer look at how far the cheapest direction on this page can be pushed before a call has to be made.

Faceted Frontier · Riff one

Vaportrail

3 rocket-boot legs, antigrav horn — chrome + neon finish

Same low-vertex rig as the base direction, but the shading leans into early-3D nostalgia instead of hiding it: glossy chrome-gradient facets, a banded vaporwave sun, and a perspective grid horizon standing in for a whole environment. The antigrav horn becomes a literal neon beacon — a pony that's part racehorse, part screensaver. The retro-tech glow reads as playful kitsch, not edgy, keeping it in Goat-Simulator territory rather than tryhard sci-fi. Build cost stays close to the base direction — same meshes, same rig — plus one gradient/fresnel facet shader and a simple grid-and-sun backdrop, so it's still a cheap reskin, just not the cheapest.

Godot build effort medium
Faceted Frontier · Riff two

Blockfoal

3 rocket-boot legs, antigrav horn — built from unit cubes

Swap flat-shaded polygons for visible unit cubes and the whole pony reads as built rather than sculpted — three blocky rocket-boot legs, a stacked-cube horn, a mane that steps like pixel stairs. It leans on instantly-familiar block-game vernacular, which does a lot of the "why is this charming" work for free, and a construction-toy pony is funny by default, so wholesome-absurd barely needs selling. It's also the cheapest thing on this whole page to build and reskin: every breeding mutation is just adding, removing, or recoloring cubes on a shared unit-cube kit — no new topology, no custom shader beyond flat/toon shading already in use elsewhere.

Godot build effort low
Faceted Frontier · Riff three

Disc Rot

3 rocket-boot legs, antigrav horn — dithered demo-disc finish

Same faceted rig again, but the render pipeline itself is the bit: ordered-dither checkerboard shading instead of smooth gradients, jittery vertex-snap wobble, faint chromatic-aberration fringing, and scanlines — the exact artifacts of a warped 1998 demo disc. It pushes "janky by design" a step further than the base direction, because the instability other styles fight to hide becomes the whole joke here, and it pairs naturally with retro framing (loading screens, fake memory-card icons) that keeps wholesome-absurd feeling like a bit, not a budget compromise. Geometry stays as cheap as the base direction, but the full effect needs real shader work — a dither post-process, a vertex-snap shader, a chromatic-aberration pass — tuned so it doesn't fight Godot's own anti-aliasing. The priciest of the three riffs, though still lighter than a full claymation build.

Godot build effort high

Reality check

What's actually in the game right now

Everything above is concept art for a decision that hasn't been made yet. This section isn't a fourth pitch — it's the exact same genome generator and rig-builder as the live prototype above, ported line-for-line to match pony_rig_builder.gd as it exists in the Godot project today, after a round of real bug-fixing rather than a style change. Two things came out of that pass: flat-shaded icosahedrons were going near-black under Godot's single directional light, because with only ~20 big faces most of them simply never point toward it, so bodies, heads, eyes and knee-caps are now smooth-shaded spheres instead; and the three-tone light/dark/main patchwork on the coat collapsed into one color per pony, because it was reading as blotchy rather than intentionally shaded. Materials also picked up a glossier clearcoat finish to stop looking flat once real lighting hit them. Here's that build, generating and animating live, sitting right below the original so it can be judged side by side instead of from memory.

Identical genome, identical spring-damper rig, identical three animation states — the only thing that changed here is exactly what changed in pony_rig_builder.gd: icosahedrons became spheres, three coat tones became one, and roughness dropped while metalness and clearcoat went up. Art direction, round two

Rounded, but read as pony — not pig

The smooth-sphere build above is honest about a real risk: round a silhouette down far enough and it stops reading as "horse" and starts reading as "hog" — a short flat snout, no jawline, a neck that's just more torso. None of these three directions retreat from rounded, soft shapes; they just spend the roundness on a longer tapered muzzle, a defined jaw and cheek, an arched neck, and slimmer tapered legs — the specific silhouette cues that keep a soft shape equine instead of porcine — while each committing to a genuinely different rendering technique.

Round two · Direction one

Comet Clay

tapered clay muzzle + jaw crease — reads pony, not pig

Same hand-molded matte clay language the studio already likes, just pointed at horse anatomy instead of a round blob: a single elongated, narrowing head shape and a jaw crease read as an actual muzzle and mouth instead of a snout, and it costs nothing extra to model over the current rounded-primitive rig — just different proportions on shapes Godot already knows how to render. The dimples and asymmetry that already sell "wholesome hand-made" also hide the seams where torso meets neck meets head, so the join never looks like three balls stacked in a trenchcoat. Cheapest of the three to actually build next.

Godot build effort low–medium
Round two · Direction two

Vinyl Voyager

3 glossy rocket-boot legs, sculpted seam — toy-shiny, still equine

Leans into glossy collectible-toy plastic instead of a game-engine material afterthought — a bold color-blocked coat, a sculpted parting-line seam across the body like a real vinyl toy's mold line, and a broad specular sheen that makes "shiny" look intentional rather than over-lit. Chunky tapered rocket-boot legs and a blunt-but-narrowing muzzle keep the toy-figure exaggeration on the horse side of the line, not the pig side — the extra mass concentrates in the body and boots, never the face. Needs a real gradient/fresnel shader and joint-seam decal work beyond what's on the rig today, so it's a bigger lift than the current build — closer to the mid-tier riffs above.

Godot build effort medium
Round two · Direction three

Starstitch

stitched muzzle seam, yarn mane + tail — plush, not piggy

Reframes "soft and rounded" as sewn instead of poured — visible dashed stitch seams where felt panels meet, a patch appliqué on the flank, yarn-loop mane and tail instead of geometry-only hair, and plain button eyes. The stitched seam at the muzzle is doing real anatomical work, not just decoration — it sits exactly where a real jaw crease would, so the taper reads as a sewn snout rather than a flat pig face. It's the priciest of the three: convincing fabric needs a fuzzy-edge shader, stitch-line decals, and yarn geometry that don't exist in the project yet.

Godot build effort high
Art direction, round three

Same roundness, three different bodies — seen from where you'll actually see them

Comet Clay, Vinyl Voyager and Starstitch were one silhouette wearing three surfaces. These three keep the rounded, soft direction and the anti-pig cues — long tapered muzzle, real jaw, an actual neck, slim tapered legs — but change the skeleton instead of the finish: where the mass sits, how long the neck is and how hard it arches, how tall and how close the legs run, how wide the rump is, and how much visual weight the tail carries. And because a racer spends the whole race staring at the back of their own pony, they're shown live, abreast, and pointed away from you — one WebGL context, three ponies, the rear read front and center. Three builds, dead astern

one proportion vector, three ponies — same builder, different numbers

All three are built by one parameterised routine — the same primitives pony_rig_builder.gd already uses (scaled spheres, tapered cylinders, a two-bone hip→knee leg) with nothing but the numbers changed. Nothing here needs new geometry code to ship; it needs a proportion block on the genome. They're trotting slightly out of phase, the way a field does, and lanes 1–3 read left to right from astern — the same order as the cards below.

Camera

Rear three-quarter — the default race camera. Rump, tail and hind-leg splay do nearly all the identifying work from here.

Lane 1

Ballast Cob

leg 1.10 · barrel 0.66w · rump 0.84w · neck 0.72 · tail ⌀0.20

A stocky draft build: deep barrel, heavy shoulder, short thick legs on a wide stance, and a short crested neck that arches hard over a small distance. It is the only one of the three that is wider at the hip than at the shoulder — the mass is packed low and back, which is also why it looks slow and reliable rather than fast, a real thing to encode in a racer's stat spread.

From behind

The rump is the pony. Two big haunch domes with a soft valley between them fill the frame, wider than anything ahead of them, so the shoulders vanish behind the hips and the low-carried head barely clears the rump line — a small pale nub a long way forward, useless as an identifier at race distance. The tail hangs low and thick in that valley, a heavy curtain carrying roughly a third of the visual mass and the only thing breaking the rump into a readable shape. The legs plant well outside the body line and splay outward, so from dead astern you read four distinct posts, not a blur.

Lane 2

Slipstream Colt

leg 1.62 · barrel 0.40w · rump 0.54w · neck 1.02 · tail ⌀0.13

Lean and leggy: a narrow slab-sided chest, a hard tuck up behind the ribs, legs half again as long as the Cob's and set close together, and a long low neck that reaches forward instead of arching up. The mane is clipped short. It is nearly twice as tall as it is wide, which is the whole silhouette in one number.

From behind

Negative space does the identifying. It's narrow enough that you see track through it — daylight under the barrel and between the legs, which the other two never give you. The haunches are two small high knobs sitting above the barrel line rather than beside it. The tail is set high and flags up and back, so it streams above the silhouette instead of covering the rump — a banner, not a curtain, and it's what you'll actually pick this pony out by. Legs track close and near-vertical, almost single-file at speed. The head is out there, but the neck reaches so far forward that from astern it's a distant speck past the shoulders.

Lane 3

Aurora Willow

leg 1.75 · barrel 0.44w · rump 0.58w · neck 1.45 · tail ⌀0.10

Tall and willowy: a short back, high withers, the longest and slimmest legs of the three converging slightly under the body, and a very long neck that arches up and folds the head back over itself. Its neck is longer than its back — an extreme proportion the current rig can't express at all, and the clearest argument for putting neck length in the genome.

From behind

The only one of the three whose head is part of the rear silhouette. The neck arches so far above a small, narrow rump that from astern you're looking at the crest, the mane running down it, and the poll — the head is the identifying feature even from behind, which no other build on this page manages. The tail is a thin ribbon reaching almost to the fetlocks, swinging across the hind legs, so it reads as motion rather than mass. The rump is doing the least work here of any of the three.

The generator, as it ships

Roll a pony the way the game rolls one

Every generator on this page above is older than the code it was describing. The genome has since grown the exaggeration genes — a leg-length gene that runs from near-vestigial stubs to absurd stilts, a body-size gene with rare tails at both ends, neck and girth multipliers — and the leg count now runs 3 to 7, laid out in rows down the barrel on a travelling-wave phase offset so a seven-legged pony ripples like a centipede instead of stomping. Nearly half of them are wearing a space helmet. This section is a direct port of pony_genome.gd, pony_rig_builder.gd and pony_controller.gd as they exist today: the same weightings, the same three body plans, the same spring constants. Hit Generate Pony and you get exactly what a race slot would get.
